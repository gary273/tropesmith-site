What "Historical Fiction" promises readers

Historical fiction in Tropesmith's romance corpus means readers asking for a period setting to carry real narrative weight — not wallpaper Regency, but history that shapes what the characters can and can't do to each other. The requests skew toward under-mined eras: readers naming specific decades and regions rather than the same three well-worn settings.

The number that matters here is supply_count: effectively zero books in the registry are tagged against this exact demand. That's a novel-gap, not an underserved lane — the difference between "more competition than the demand justifies" and "nobody has shown up yet." It's the more valuable signal of the two, and the riskier one: thin supply can mean an open lane or it can mean the demand is real but too diffuse for any one book to satisfy. Read it as a lead to research, not a guarantee.

What readers reject in romance

Demand tells you what readers want written. Rejection tells you how they'll punish it if you get the execution wrong. Tropesmith mines 1–2★ Goodreads reviews for the specific reason a reader gave up, then classifies it against a fixed list of dealbreaker patterns:

How to use this before you draft

Read the three numbers above together, not separately. 42,923 requests tells you the trope is wanted at real scale; 100% underserved tells you how much of that want has no matching tagged supply yet; and the 15% recent-request share tells you whether that gap is closing or holding steady right now. A high request count with low underserved means the trope is proven but the shelf has largely caught up — the opportunity is in a specific unclaimed angle, not the trope's bare existence. A high underserved figure means more room, but pair it with the rejection data above: readers punish weak execution of a trope they want just as fast as they ignore one they don't.
