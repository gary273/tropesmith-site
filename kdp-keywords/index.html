KDP Keywords for Authors: A Data-Backed Approach

Written 22 August 2026 · Per-trope counts as of 7 October 2026 · Source: Tropesmith demand-signal database

Your KDP keywords are the seven phrases that tell Amazon who your book is for. Get them right and the store surfaces you to the readers already looking; get them wrong and even a great book stays invisible. This guide covers how Amazon KDP keywords work — and the part most guides skip: how to pick them from demand signals instead of guesswork.

A demand signal is one specific thing a reader asked for or praised in a review, comment or post, picked out by Tropesmith's classifier. One review can give several. Each signal gets a rarity tag (common, underserved or novel) from the text of that review or post alone; the classifier is never shown what is on the shelf. A tag points at where to look. Short answer

Use a keyword tool that reads reader demand, not just Amazon's autocomplete. The established choices are Publisher Rocket and the free keyword guides from Kindlepreneur, which both work from Amazon search-volume estimates. Tropesmith takes the other half of the problem: it counts how many demand signals mention each of the 2,673 tropes in its trope list that at least one demand signal mentions, so you can see how often readers mention the tropes behind each phrase. Volume tells you how many people search a phrase; demand signals tell you how often readers mention the trope. Use both.

What KDP keywords actually are

When you publish on Amazon KDP you get seven keyword slots. They are not single words — each slot takes a phrase, so “enemies to lovers hockey romance” is one keyword, not three. Amazon uses them, alongside your categories, title and description, to decide which searches and also-boughts your book shows up in. They are one of the few levers you fully control after you hit publish, and you can edit them any time.

How Amazon KDP keywords work

Two ideas do most of the work. First, relevance: a keyword only helps if your book genuinely delivers what it promises — Amazon rewards clicks that turn into reads, not just impressions. Second, reachability: a phrase readers actually type. The sweet spot for KDP keywords is a phrase with real search demand that also matches your book precisely, so you rank for terms you can realistically win rather than broad terms buried under bestsellers.

Common mistakes waste slots: repeating words already in your title or subtitle (Amazon indexes those anyway), stuffing single generic words like “romance,” using another author's name, or picking aspirational phrases your book does not actually satisfy. Every wasted slot is a search you have chosen not to appear in.

The part most guides miss: pick keywords from demand

Most keyword advice stops at “brainstorm phrases and check the Amazon search bar.” The problem is that the obvious phrases are the crowded ones. The better move is to add what readers themselves say: the trope phrases that show up in their reviews, comments and posts.

That is what Tropesmith's demand data is for. Tropesmith tags the 2,591,893 demand signals counted on 2026-10-07 at 17:24 UTC with the tropes they mention and counts how many mention each one; the signals come from Goodreads reviews, Amazon reviews, BookTok comments and captions, Reddit posts and Amazon Q&A. Enemies-to-lovers, for instance, appears in 59,379 demand signals, with 32% tagged underserved or novel by Tropesmith's classifier; dark romance appears in 40,738, with 49% tagged. A high count tells you readers mention the phrase often. The tag is judged from each text alone, with no view of the shelf, so check the comp titles to see how crowded a phrase is.

A simple way to choose your seven

Anchor two or three slots to your strongest trope stack (the dynamics your book truly delivers), using demand-signal counts to see which of your tropes readers mention most. Use one or two slots for subgenre and setting, one for reader-intent phrases like “spicy” or “slow burn,” and keep one flexible to test and swap. Then revisit after a few weeks — KDP keywords are editable, so treat them as a dial, not a one-time decision.

Why demand-backed keywords help

Anyone can guess keywords. The edge is choosing trope phrases that readers actually use in their reviews, comments and posts, then checking the shelf with comp titles. That is where a KDP keyword slot earns its place.

How this compares to the keyword tools you have heard of

Most authors researching book keywords land on one of three approaches. They answer different questions, and the honest advice is that they stack rather than replace each other.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best tool for KDP keywords?

There is no single best tool, because keyword research has two halves. Search-volume tools such as Publisher Rocket estimate how many people search a phrase and how many books compete for it. Tropesmith covers the other half: how often readers mention a trope, drawn from the 2,591,893 demand signals counted on 2026-10-07, so you can see which trope phrases readers use most. Authors who take this seriously use both, plus Amazon's own autocomplete as a free starting point.

Is there a free way to research book keywords?

Yes. Amazon's own search bar autocomplete is free and shows you real phrases shoppers type, and Tropesmith publishes free demand pages, the romance tropes list, book tropes by genre and BookTok books, ranked by demand signals. Start free, and only pay for a tool once you know which decision you are trying to make.

What are KDP keywords?

KDP keywords are the seven phrase slots Amazon gives you when you publish a book. Amazon uses them, with your categories and description, to decide which searches your book appears in. Each slot holds a full phrase, and you can edit them at any time.

How many Amazon KDP keywords do you get?

Seven. Each of the seven slots takes a phrase rather than a single word, so you can target things like 'enemies to lovers small town romance' as one keyword. Words already in your title and subtitle are indexed automatically, so don't waste slots repeating them.
