Book tropes by genre: what each shelf is asking for

Tropes are not evenly distributed. The same four-week window, split by genre family, shows each shelf has its own leaderboard — and that a handful of tropes (found family, slow burn) show up on nearly all of them.

Fantasy book tropes (13 lanes · 4,184 signals)

Found family leads by a distance (220 signals), then slow burn (94), paranormal romance (81), fated mates (79), reverse harem (70), enemies-to-lovers (63), dark fantasy (58) and LitRPG (55). Fantasy readers want the crew as much as the quest — and romantasy keeps pulling romance tropes across the aisle.

Horror book tropes (7 lanes · 3,345 signals)

Horror runs on mode, not relationship: body horror (124), psychological horror (110), atmospheric horror (84), cosmic horror (76), gothic horror (55), unreliable narrator (49) and folk horror (47). Note slow burn at 48 — dread is a pacing trope too.

Mystery book tropes (18 lanes · 2,480 signals)

Cozy mystery dominates (210), with found family second (105) — readers want their sleuth to have people. Then mystery-suspense (70), whodunit (62) and amateur sleuth (49). Mystery is the widest genre family we track at 18 distinct lanes.

Thriller book tropes (12 lanes · 2,073 signals)

Unreliable narrator leads (83), then mystery-suspense (67), psychological thriller (62), plot twists (42) and twist ending (36). Thriller demand is structural: readers are asking to be fooled, well.

Science fiction book tropes (10 lanes · 1,167 signals)

Found family again (59), then slow burn (23), first contact (23), space opera (19), survival (19), post-apocalyptic (17) and dystopian (14). The warmest trope in fiction tops the coldest setting.

Historical fiction book tropes (8 lanes · 951 signals)

After the genre's own label, found family (50) and dual timeline (28) lead, with slow burn (22), forbidden romance (12) and coming-of-age (12) behind. Dual timeline is the one structural trope that belongs to this shelf more than any other.

Romance book tropes (67 lanes · 30,400 signals)

Romance is the biggest trope market by far — over two-thirds of all reader signals we track. It has its own dedicated, deeper breakdown: romance tropes ranked by demand and the full romance tropes list.

How to read this

Two numbers matter for positioning a book: how loudly a trope is being requested (signals), and how widely it travels (lanes). Across Tropesmith's full corpus of 767,210 tracked reader requests, 95% are flagged as underserved — readers are asking for more than the shelf currently supplies. The gap is the opportunity.

Tropes travel; genres don't

The most useful thing this data shows: readers carry their favourite tropes across shelves. Found family and slow burn chart in all seven genre families. Enemies-to-lovers charts in six. Unreliable narrator — born in literary fiction — now tops thriller demand and ranks in horror. If your book combines a genre with the tropes its readers are importing, you inherit demand from every shelf those tropes chart on.

Tropes rarely work alone

Most published fiction stacks three to five tropes — a fantasy with found family + slow burn + forced proximity, a thriller with unreliable narrator + dual timeline. Frequently asked questions

What are book tropes?

A book trope is a recognisable story pattern readers seek out on purpose — a relationship dynamic (enemies-to-lovers), a structural device (unreliable narrator, dual timeline), a mood (cozy mystery, cosmic horror) or a setup (forced proximity, marriage of convenience). Tropes are how readers find the exact reading experience they want, which is why they matter for positioning a book.

What are the most popular book tropes right now?

Across every genre Tropesmith tracks, the most-requested book tropes in the last four weeks are found family (1,671 reader signals), slow burn (1,600), forced proximity (1,134), enemies-to-lovers (1,094) and dark romance (851). Found family and slow burn appear in all seven genre families — the closest thing fiction has to universal tropes.

How many book tropes are there?

There is no fixed number, and any site quoting one is guessing. Tropesmith's trope taxonomy holds 2,059 named tropes; 2,015 of them carry live reader demand, and 1,617 were requested by readers in the last four weeks alone. What matters for an author is not the count — it is which tropes readers are asking for right now in your genre, which is what this page ranks.

Are tropes the same as genres?

No. A genre (mystery, fantasy, romance) is the shelf a book sits on; a trope is a pattern inside the story. Tropes travel between genres — found family tops the demand chart in fantasy, sci-fi, mystery and historical fiction, and slow burn charts in all seven families we track. Most books combine a genre with three to five tropes.

How do I choose tropes for my book?

Start from demand, not from a static list: pick tropes readers in your genre are actively requesting, then check how crowded the supply is. A trope with high demand and thin supply is an open lane. How should I approach book tropes?

Start from demand, not from a static list. Tropesmith names 2,059 book tropes and 2,015 of them carry live reader demand, read from 44,600 reader signals in the last four weeks. Pick the tropes readers in your genre are actively asking for, then check how crowded the supply already is: high demand with thin supply is an open lane, high demand with heavy supply is a queue. Tropes travel between genres, so a pattern topping the chart in fantasy is not spent in yours — and a trope you choose because it is on a list, rather than because readers are asking for it, is a guess wearing a label.

Compared with the tools authors usually reach for. Publisher Rocket reports Amazon keyword and category data, KDSpy reads bestseller pages for a quick niche verdict, BookBeam tracks how published books perform over time, and K-lytics writes up a genre as of its report date. All four describe the shelf that already exists. None of them counts what readers asked for and did not get — which is the only number on this page.
