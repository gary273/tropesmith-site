Book Tropes: What They Are and Which Ones Readers Want, by Genre

Updated 17 October 2026 · Definitions from Tropesmith's book tropes list · Demand questions are answered by the free tools linked below, which show their own live data

Book tropes are recognizable story patterns, such as enemies to lovers, found family or an unreliable narrator, that readers look for on purpose. This page defines the main types with examples from Tropesmith's book tropes list, then shows how to see which tropes readers in your own genre want.

What are the main types of book tropes?

This page groups book tropes into five types, which helps when you are choosing a stack for a book. Definitions are from our book tropes list.

TypeWhat it describesExamples and what they mean
Relationship dynamicsHow two characters relate and change.Enemies to lovers: antagonists become allies become everything. Friends to lovers: the friendship was the love story all along. Second chance: old flames get the reunion the first attempt never earned. Slow burn: attraction builds slowly on purpose. Found family: a chosen group becomes a real family, bound by loyalty rather than blood.
Setups and settingsThe situation or place that drives the story.Forced proximity: circumstances trap characters together until they must deal with each other. Marriage of convenience: a practical arrangement ambushed by real feelings. Fake dating: a pretend relationship that forgets it is pretending. Small town: a close-knit community that is practically a character.
Character typesThe kind of lead the reader is promised.Morally gray (or grey) MMC: a male lead whose ethics readers argue about. Protective MMC: a male lead who stands between his person and the world. Grumpy and sunshine: relentless warmth versus committed grump.
Story structures and devicesHow the story is told.Unreliable narrator: the voice telling the story cannot be trusted, and that is the point. Plot twists: reversals that reframe everything the reader thought they knew. Ensemble cast: the story belongs to a group, not a single lead.
Moods and subgenresThe feel of the read.Cozy mystery: gentle crime-solving, low gore, high community. Dark romance: a love story that runs on danger, obsession and broken rules. Paranormal romance: romance where one heart doesn't beat quite human.

The full set of 40 tropes, each with a plain definition, is on the book tropes list. Romance has its own deeper page: the romance tropes list.

Are book tropes the same as genres?

No. A genre, such as mystery, fantasy or romance, is the shelf a book sits on, and a trope is a pattern inside the story. A few labels do both: cozy mystery and paranormal romance are subgenres, and also tropes readers ask for. Tropes travel between genres: found family and slow burn turn up in romance, fantasy and mystery alike. Most books combine a genre with several tropes, which is why readers often ask for two at once.

What are examples of book tropes by genre?

In romance, enemies to lovers and fake dating. In fantasy, found family and romantasy. In thrillers, the unreliable narrator and the twist ending. Only interested in romance? The romance tropes pages have one for each romance trope.

Trope vs cliche vs archetype

A trope is a pattern readers choose. A cliche is a trope used so automatically that it no longer surprises, and the same trope can be either depending on how it is written. An archetype is a type of character, such as the mentor or the rogue, rather than a plot pattern or a setup. Tropes are often built from archetypes: grumpy sunshine, for example, pairs two of them.

How do I choose book tropes for my own book?

Choose tropes readers in your genre are asking for, then check how crowded each one is. Demand without a supply gap is a crowd, not an opening.

  1. Name your genre and subgenre first, because tropes behave differently on each shelf, then read a few books in it that lean on the tropes you are considering.
  2. Pick one lead trope readers ask for by name, and one that gives your book its own angle.
  3. Check each in the Trope Demand Checker (and, for romance, the Romance Trope Opportunity Check) to see how much readers mention it and which way its share is moving, then look at the comp titles to see how many books already carry it.
  4. Check the pairing in the Trope Pair Finder, which sets demand signals against tagged titles.
  5. Test the whole stack in a Tropesmith Map, which ranks tropes for your romance subgenre by demand against supply and notes the crowded ones to skip or subvert.

Frequently asked questions

What are book tropes?

Book tropes are recognizable story patterns that readers seek out on purpose: a relationship dynamic such as enemies to lovers, a setup such as forced proximity, a device such as an unreliable narrator, or a mood such as cozy mystery. They are how readers find the exact reading experience they want.

What are the most popular book tropes?

It depends on the genre, so there is no single ranking. Found family, slow burn, forced proximity and enemies to lovers turn up across many genres. For a counted answer in your own genre, use the free Trope Demand Checker.

Are tropes the same as genres?

No. A genre is the shelf a book sits on, and a trope is a pattern inside the story. Tropes travel between genres, and most books combine one genre with several tropes.

How many book tropes are there?

There is no fixed number, and any page that quotes one is counting its own list. Tropesmith's book tropes list defines 40, and the Trope Demand Checker covers more by genre.

How do I choose tropes for my book?

Start from your genre, pick tropes readers there ask for, then check how crowded each one is. A trope with strong demand and little supply is an opening, and strong demand with heavy supply is a queue. A Tropesmith Map ranks the tropes for your romance subgenre on exactly that basis.

Where can I see a full list of book tropes?

Tropesmith's book tropes list gives 40 tropes with a plain definition each, and the romance tropes list goes deeper on romance. Both link to the free tools that show demand by genre.
