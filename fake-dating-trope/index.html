What the fake dating trope promises

Two people agree to pretend. A wedding needs a plus-one, a grandmother needs reassuring, a promotion needs a stable partner, a visa needs a marriage. The audience must be convinced, so the pretence demands closeness: hand-holding that lingers, a shared hotel room, a rehearsed backstory that starts to feel like a memory. The promise to the reader is the slow collapse of the performance — the moment one of them forgets they are acting, and the terrifying question of whether the other has too.

It is a contemporary staple first, but the mechanism travels: a fake betrothal to stop a war in romantasy, a cover-story couple in romantic suspense, a fake mate bond in paranormal. Our data shows readers asking for it in 6 genre families. A second engine tag, fake relationship, catches the broader version (fake engagement, fake marriage) and adds 510 requests; together the two tags total 2,275.

What readers say they want from it

Reading the highest-strength requests themselves: the single most repeated ask is fake dating combined with enemies-to-lovers — readers describe the pairing as the ideal and say choosing one is impossible. They want the reason for the pretence to be believable and high-stakes (a PR stunt to rescue two public images gets named; "just convenient" gets criticised), and they want the arrangement to escalate into real drama rather than fizzle. Setting requests are specific: Formula 1 and other sports worlds, workplace forced proximity, holiday settings with real heat, and queer fake dating. And across the thread runs a quieter ask — a fake-relationship hero who is genuinely kind, comforting and empowering, not a bad boy in disguise. 15% of the requests naming this trope come from BookTok.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fake dating trope?

The fake dating trope is a romance where two people agree to pretend they are a couple — to satisfy a family, win a bet, make an ex jealous, secure a visa or a promotion — and the pretence becomes real. The engine of the trope is the gap between the public performance and the private feelings, and the moment one of them stops acting.

How popular is fake dating?

Tropesmith logs 1,765 individual reader requests naming fake dating, plus 510 tagged as fake relationship, with 96% of the demand flagged as underserved. It shows reader demand in 73 genre lanes across 6 genre families — overwhelmingly contemporary, but with a real following in romantasy and paranormal where the arrangement is a political alliance or a cover story.

What is the difference between fake dating and marriage of convenience?

Fake dating is a pretence with an exit: the couple performs a relationship for an audience and can, in theory, walk away. Marriage of convenience is a real legal contract entered for practical reasons. Both run on the same fuel — forced intimacy that turns sincere — but marriage of convenience carries higher stakes and is more common in historical romance, while fake dating dominates contemporary and rom-com.

What tropes go well with fake dating?

Readers stack fake dating most often with forced proximity (the one-bed scene at the family wedding), enemies-to-lovers or grumpy-sunshine (the partner you'd least choose), friends-to-lovers (the friend who agrees to help) and he-falls-first. The common thread is a reason the performance has to be convincing, which forces real closeness.

Does the fake dating trope sell?

The demand is real and under-supplied: 1,765 reader requests at 96% underserved. It is also one of the most crowded premises in contemporary romance, so the lane you place it in and the stack you pair it with decide whether a given book finds air.
