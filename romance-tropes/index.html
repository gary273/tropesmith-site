A romance trope is a recurring story pattern that readers look for on purpose, such as enemies to lovers, slow burn or fake dating. Readers browse, shelve and buy by trope. Closeness tropes: forced proximity, friends to lovers, found family

These put two people, or a whole chosen family, close together and let feeling grow from the closeness. forced proximity traps the leads in one place, friends to lovers turns a safe bond into a risk, second chance romance gives former partners a second try, and found family widens the circle beyond the couple.

Friction tropes: enemies to lovers, grumpy sunshine, forbidden romance

Friction tropes run on opposition. enemies to lovers turns rivalry into attraction (see the enemies to lovers books list), grumpy sunshine pairs a guarded lead with a warm one, and forbidden romance makes the wall around the couple the whole story.

Contract and fate tropes: fake dating, marriage of convenience, fated mates

These start the relationship by arrangement or by destiny rather than by choice. fake dating and marriage of convenience begin with a deal; fated mates begins with a bond the leads did not pick.

Heat and tone: slow burn, dark romance, reverse harem, age gap

Some tropes set the pace and the temperature rather than the situation. slow burn holds the payoff back, dark romance raises the danger, reverse harem gives the lead more than one love interest, and age gap shapes the power balance between the leads. Setting tropes sit alongside them: paranormal romance, historical romance and small town.

Romantasy tropes

Romantasy blends romance with fantasy, and its tropes lean on found family, fated mates and enemies to lovers. The romantasy tropes page ranks them by demand signals in the latest month of the trend table, and the romantasy market snapshot covers price and competition.

How to read this

A high demand-signal count means readers mention a trope often in reviews, comments and posts, whether wanting it or praising it. The tag is judged from each review or post alone, with no view of what is on the shelf, so it points at where to look. It does not prove that no book answers the signal. To see what already sits on the shelf, check the comp titles for your trope stack or look at a sample Map.

Why romance tropes matter for authors

Readers browse, shelve and buy by trope. A book that names the tropes readers mention — in its blurb, keywords and cover — meets readers where they are already searching. But popularity alone can mislead: a trope many readers mention is also a trope many authors write. Counting demand signals tells you how often readers mention a trope; the comp titles for your stack show how crowded its shelf already is. For the method behind it, see how to read demand signals and how to research tropes before you write.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most popular romance tropes?

Of the 17 romance tropes on this list, Found Family has the most demand signals (300,602, or 11.6% of all signals), followed by Slow Burn (125,290) and Forced Proximity (125,150). A demand signal is one specific thing a reader asked for or praised in a review, comment or post; counted 2026-10-07 17:24 UTC.

What is a romance trope?

A romance trope is a recurring, recognisable pattern readers actively seek out: a relationship setup, dynamic or premise like enemies to lovers, grumpy sunshine or fated mates. Readers browse and buy by trope, which is why matching the tropes in demand is one of the strongest levers an author has.

How many romance tropes are there?

There is no fixed number, and any site quoting one is guessing. As of 8 October 2026, Tropesmith's trope list holds 754 romance tropes (426 live, 328 candidates) and 2,711 across all genres; 2,673 of those 2,711 appear as a main or secondary tag on at least one demand signal (counted on 2026-10-07). This list covers 17 of them, each with its own page.

How we count

A demand signal is one specific thing a reader asked for or praised in a review, comment or post, picked out by Tropesmith’s classifier. One review can give several. Tropesmith had analyzed 2,591,893 of them on 2026-10-07 17:24 UTC; 95.6% of those come from Goodreads and Amazon reviews, 4.1% from BookTok comments and captions and 0.3% from Reddit posts. Each signal also gets a rarity tag (common, underserved or novel) from the text of that review or post alone; the classifier is never shown what is on the shelf, so a tag points at where to look and does not prove that no book answers the signal. 39% of all signals are tagged underserved or novel by Tropesmith’s classifier.

The shares in the table are explained under the table. The 17 tropes in the table are a fixed set, not re-picked from today’s counts, so tropes with more signals than some rows here, such as Banter and Coming Of Age, are not shown; genre and craft labels and duplicate tags (Why Choose, Slow Burn Romance) were left out.
