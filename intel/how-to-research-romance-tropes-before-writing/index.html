How to research romance tropes before you write a word

Updated 13 August 2026 · ~8 min read

Tropes are the units romance readers shop in. They don't browse for “a well-written love story” — they reach for enemies-to-lovers, for grumpy-sunshine, for fated mates, and they can tell from a cover and three blurb lines whether your book delivers the one they're craving. Which means the tropes you build your book on are a market decision as much as a creative one — and worth an afternoon of research before you write a word.

This is not a trope glossary. It's the research method: how to find out which tropes are working in your lane, which are oversupplied, and how to assemble a stack readers actually want.

First, pick your lane

Trope demand is lane-specific. A trope surging in romantasy can be background noise in small-town contemporary; what's fresh in dark romance may be worn out in mafia romance next door. So fix your subgenre first — if you haven't, start with what romance subgenres sell — and research tropes inside that lane only. Every free Tropesmith lane snapshot names the lane's fastest-rising trope, which is a live starting point: see what's rising right now in enemies-to-lovers contemporary or paranormal romance.

The method

STEP 1

List the lane's working tropes from its bestsellers

Open your subgenre's current bestseller chart and read the packaging — covers, titles, blurbs — of the top books. Write down every trope being promised (not every trope that appears in the text; the promise is what sells). After twenty books you'll have the lane's working vocabulary: the tropes readers in this lane are being sold, and roughly how often.

STEP 2

Now read demand: what readers ask for

Supply tells you what publishers and indies think readers want. Demand is what readers say they want — in Reddit request threads, BookTok comments, and Goodreads reviews. Collect the “I want a book that…” and “why is no one writing…” sentences that mention your lane. Our guide to reader demand signals covers where to look and how to weigh what you find.

STEP 3

Sort every trope into one of three buckets

Cross-reference the two lists. Each trope lands in a bucket: hot (high demand, high supply — safe but competitive), undersupplied (readers asking, shelf thin — the opportunity bucket), or tired (heavy supply, cooling demand — the bucket that quietly sinks books). This demand-vs-supply comparison is the heart of trope research, and it's exactly what a Tropesmith Map renders as ranked bars for your lane.

STEP 4

Mine reviews for the gap inside the trope

Reviews of trope-matched bestsellers tell you how the trope is being delivered — and where readers are unsatisfied. “I wanted more groveling,” “the third-act breakup ruined it,” “wish the heroine had teeth.” Those complaints are your differentiation, spoken by the exact readers you're targeting.

STEP 5

Build the stack: one lead, a few supports

A saleable romance is rarely one trope; it's a stack — a lead trope that does the promising on the cover and blurb, supported by a small number of complementary tropes that deepen the read. Lead with your strongest hot-or-undersupplied trope; support with tropes that share its audience. A Tropesmith Map recommends a five-trope stack for exactly this reason: enough to make the book feel rich, few enough that the promise stays legible.

STEP 6

Sanity-check the stack against real comps

If your stack is genuinely wanted, books carrying most of it should exist and be doing respectably — your comps. If nothing on the shelf resembles your stack, be honest about which you've found: a gap readers are asking for, or a combination readers don't want. The demand evidence from step 2 is how you tell the difference. See how to find comparable titles for the full comp method.

The principle

Research the promise, not the plot. Readers buy the trope promise on the outside of the book; deliver it however your story demands on the inside.

Frequently asked questions

How many tropes should a romance novel have?

Think in terms of a stack: one lead trope that carries the cover-and-blurb promise, supported by a few complementary tropes that share its audience. A Tropesmith Map recommends a five-trope stack — rich enough to feel layered, small enough that the promise stays instantly legible.

Should I write a trope I don't enjoy just because it's in demand?

No. Readers can feel a resented trope, and you'll be living with it for months. The method exists to find the overlap between what readers are reaching for and what you'd genuinely love to write — that overlap is where books get finished and sell.

What does demand vs supply mean for tropes?

Demand is how much readers are asking for a trope (BookTok, Reddit requests, reviews); supply is how many current books in the lane deliver it. High demand with thin supply marks an opportunity; heavy supply with cooling demand marks a trope likely to underperform.
