Romance comps: how to find comparable titles that hold up

Updated 13 August 2026 · ~8 min read

Comparable titles — comps — usually get discussed as a pitching formality or an ad-targeting trick. Both undersell them. Found properly, comps are the densest market evidence available to a romance author: a handful of books that tell you how big your audience is, what it pays, how it expects to be packaged, and what it's still hungry for. This guide is about finding and using comps as intelligence; for choosing which comps to name in your marketing, our earlier guide how to choose comp titles covers that side in depth.

What makes a title truly comparable

A comp shares your book's audience, which in romance means it shares three things: the lane (subgenre shelf), the lead trope promise, and the heat level. Plot resemblance is optional — two books with near-identical plots can serve different readers, while two wildly different stories with the same trope-and-temperature promise share a readership almost perfectly. Recency matters too: you want books whose readers are still active and buying, not beloved titles whose moment has passed.

Where to hunt

SOURCE 1

Your lane's bestseller charts

Start on the subgenre shelf you're claiming and read the current charts — not just the top five megahits, but the healthy mid-list beneath them. Skip the outliers: a runaway phenomenon behaves like nothing else in the market and teaches you nothing about your launch. The mid-list winners are the books whose performance a new release can realistically echo.

SOURCE 2

Also-boughts and “customers also read”

Once you have one strong candidate, its store page points at the rest: the books its buyers also bought are, by definition, books your target audience buys. Two or three hops through these trails maps a readership faster than any keyword search.

SOURCE 3

Reader-curated lists and shelves

Goodreads shelves and listopia-style lists are readers doing your comp research for you — grouping books by the exact feel-and-trope criteria that define a true comp. If your book belongs on a shelf readers have already named, every other book on that shelf is a candidate.

SOURCE 4

BookTok and request threads

Titles recommended together in BookTok comments and Reddit “books like X” threads reveal the associations in readers' heads — which is what a comp really is. These sources also surface rising books charts haven't caught yet, which make the freshest comps of all. (More on reading these: reader demand signals.)

Vetting the candidates

Run every candidate through four filters: lane match (same shelf your book will claim), promise match (lead trope and heat — the two axes readers actually shop on), recency (an active readership), and scale sanity (doing well without being an outlier). Two to four survivors are plenty; one is fragile, ten means you haven't chosen.

Now read them as market data

This is the step most authors skip, and it's where comps turn into intelligence:

  • Pricing and distribution. What do your comps charge, and are they in Kindle Unlimited? Their choices sketch your lane's economics — check them against the typical price and KU share on your lane's live snapshot, and see Kindle Unlimited vs wide for the decision itself.
  • Packaging language. Covers and titles across your comp set show the conventions their (your) readers trust — the baseline your own packaging should match before differentiating. This feeds directly into positioning.
  • Categories. The shelves your comps rank on are the shelves your book can plausibly claim. Tropesmith's free category checker shows every category any Amazon book ranks in, from live data.
  • The gap in the reviews. Read your comps' critical reviews for recurring wishes — “I wanted more of…”, “if only the ending had…”. Those are your differentiation instructions, written by the readers you're about to court.
Borrow / skip

For each comp, write one line of what to borrow (the choice its readers rewarded) and one of what to skip (the choice its reviews punish). Comps stop being name-drops and become a build spec.

Frequently asked questions

How many comp titles does a romance novel need?

Two to four well-vetted comps are plenty. One comp is fragile — if it's a fluke, so is your read of the market — and a list of ten means you haven't really chosen. Each comp should match your lane, lead trope promise, and heat level, and have a still-active readership.

Should I use bestsellers as comp titles?

Use the healthy mid-list of your subgenre, not the runaway megahits. Outlier phenomena behave like nothing else in the market, so they neither predict your launch nor target ads precisely. Mid-list winners have real, reachable audiences whose behaviour your book can actually echo.

What can comp titles tell me besides marketing targeting?

Read as market data, comps reveal your lane's pricing norms and Kindle Unlimited habits, the packaging conventions readers trust, the categories your book can rank in, and — through their critical reviews — the gaps readers still want filled. That's a market brief, sampled from a handful of books.
