Romance reader demand signals: what they are and how to read them

Updated 7 October 2026 · ~7 min read

Sales charts tell you what readers bought. Demand signals tell you what readers asked for and praised in reviews, comments and posts. Learning to read those signals is the closest thing publishing has to seeing around a corner, and it's the layer of market research most authors never touch.

This guide covers what the signals are, where each one lives, how to weigh them against each other, and how to do the reading yourself — plus how Tropesmith automates it at scale.

The four signal sources

1. BookTok virality

When a trope, aesthetic, or title catches on BookTok, the play counts are enormous and the effect on sales is real — entire subgenres have been lifted by it. The signal to read isn't just which books are going viral but which promises: the tropes and feelings named in captions and comments.

2. Reddit request threads

Reddit's romance communities are full of request threads: “I want a book where…”, “looking for something like X but with Y…”. This is demand in its purest form — a reader explicitly describing a book they cannot find. When the same ask recurs across threads, that is a premise worth checking against the shelf.

3. Goodreads shelves and reviews

The shelves readers create are a folksonomy of how readers actually categorise books — the exact vocabulary they use, which is worth more to your blurb and keywords than any thesaurus. Reviews add the verdict layer: what readers of a book loved, and what they wished it had done. A recurring “I wish…” in a subgenre's reviews is a quieter, more considered cousin of a Reddit request thread.

4. Amazon charts as the cross-check

Strictly, Amazon shows supply and its performance — but it's the indispensable cross-check. Demand without a supply check can send you chasing something the market already delivered, so check how many books already carry a signal before you act on it.

How to weigh conflicting signals

The sources disagree constantly, so read them side by side rather than trusting any one. Of Tropesmith's demand signals, 95.6% come from Goodreads and Amazon reviews, 4.1% from BookTok comments and captions and 0.3% from Reddit posts, so a premise that also shows up on BookTok and Reddit is worth a second look. Then cross-reference it against Amazon supply to see how many books already carry it. You can do the same by hand: a trope that's viral on BookTok, requested on Reddit, and thin on the bestseller shelf is worth checking first.

Reading tip

Weigh repetition over volume. One viral video is an event; the same craving surfacing across BookTok comments, Reddit posts, and Goodreads reviews is a market.

Doing it yourself: a weekly half-hour

HABIT 1

Follow your lane's hashtags

Pick the BookTok hashtags for your subgenre and skim weekly. Read comments, not just videos — comments are where readers say what they want next.

HABIT 2

Search the request threads

Search romance book-recommendation communities for your lane's keywords, sorted by new. Save every ask that matches your territory; recurring phrasings are the finds.

HABIT 3

Read fresh reviews of your comps

Newest reviews of comparable titles (see finding comps) tell you where current readers are delighted and where they're still hungry.

Frequently asked questions

What are reader demand signals?

Public evidence of what readers want before sales data can show it: BookTok virality around tropes and aesthetics, direct requests in Reddit romance communities, and the shelves and reviews readers create on Goodreads. Read together and checked against Amazon supply, they show what readers are talking about and how many books already carry it.

Where do Tropesmith's demand signals come from?

Goodreads reviews, Amazon reviews, BookTok comments and captions, Reddit posts and Amazon Q&A. 95.6% come from Goodreads and Amazon reviews, 4.1% from BookTok comments and captions and 0.3% from Reddit posts (source shares counted 7 October 2026).
