Romance reader demand signals: what they are and how to read them

Updated 13 August 2026 · ~7 min read

Sales charts tell you what readers bought last month. Demand signals tell you what they're reaching for next — the requests, obsessions, and complaints readers broadcast in public, every day, before the books they're describing exist. Learning to read those signals is the closest thing publishing has to seeing around a corner, and it's the layer of market research most authors never touch.

This guide covers what the signals are, where each one lives, how to weigh them against each other, and how to do the reading yourself — plus how Tropesmith automates it at scale.

The four signal sources

1. BookTok virality

When a trope, aesthetic, or title catches on BookTok, the play counts are enormous and the effect on sales is real — entire subgenres have been lifted by it. The signal to read isn't just which books are going viral but which promises: the tropes and feelings named in captions and comments. BookTok is the loudest and fastest-moving of the demand signals, which is why Tropesmith's engine weights it highest.

2. Direct reader asks

Reddit's romance communities are full of request threads: “I want a book where…”, “looking for something like X but with Y…”. This is demand in its purest form — a reader explicitly describing a book they cannot find. When the same ask recurs across threads, you're looking at a gap on the shelf, described by the people who'd buy the book that fills it.

3. Goodreads shelves and reviews

The shelves readers create are a folksonomy of how readers actually categorise books — the exact vocabulary they use, which is worth more to your blurb and keywords than any thesaurus. Reviews add the verdict layer: what readers of a book loved, and what they wished it had done. A recurring “I wish…” in a subgenre's reviews is a quieter, more considered cousin of the Reddit ask.

4. Amazon charts as the cross-check

Strictly, Amazon shows supply and its performance — but it's the indispensable cross-check. Demand without a supply check can send you chasing something the market already delivered; a signal is only an opportunity when the shelf hasn't caught up. This demand-vs-supply cross-referencing is the mechanism behind every Tropesmith verdict.

How to weigh conflicting signals

The sources disagree constantly, so you need a hierarchy. Tropesmith's engine uses this one: BookTok signals weigh most — a viral video carrying millions of plays is the strongest social proof in the corpus — then direct Reddit asks (explicit, unambiguous requests), then Goodreads reviews (single-reader praise or complaint). Whatever survives that weighing gets cross-referenced against Amazon supply to see whether the market has already answered it. You can apply the same hierarchy by hand: a trope that's viral on BookTok, requested on Reddit, and thin on the bestseller shelf is the strongest signal an author can find.

Reading tip

Weigh repetition over volume. One viral video is an event; the same craving surfacing across BookTok comments, Reddit asks, and Goodreads reviews is a market.

Doing it yourself: a weekly half-hour

HABIT 1

Follow your lane's hashtags

Pick the BookTok hashtags for your subgenre and skim weekly. Read comments, not just videos — comments are where readers say what they want next.

HABIT 2

Search the request threads

Search romance book-recommendation communities for your lane's keywords, sorted by new. Save every ask that matches your territory; recurring phrasings are the finds.

HABIT 3

Read fresh reviews of your comps

Newest reviews of comparable titles (see finding comps) tell you where current readers are delighted and where they're still hungry.

Frequently asked questions

What are reader demand signals?

Public evidence of what readers want before sales data can show it: BookTok virality around tropes and aesthetics, direct requests in Reddit romance communities, and the shelves and reviews readers create on Goodreads. Read together and checked against Amazon supply, they reveal gaps between what readers are asking for and what the shelf delivers.

Which demand signal matters most?

Tropesmith's engine weights BookTok highest — a viral video carrying millions of plays is the strongest social-proof signal in the corpus — followed by explicit Reddit asks, then Goodreads reviews, always cross-referenced against what's over-supplied on Amazon. Repetition across sources beats volume in any single one.
