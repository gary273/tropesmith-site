How to position a romance novel so readers recognise it

Updated 13 August 2026 · ~8 min read

Positioning is the promise a stranger can parse in three seconds: what lane this book lives in, which tropes it delivers, how hot it runs, and how it will make them feel. Readers decide from the thumbnail and the first blurb lines whether your book is for them — and a wonderful novel positioned vaguely loses to an average one positioned sharply, every time, because the average one gets opened.

The good news: positioning is a checklist, not a talent. Here it is.

STEP 1

Choose the shelf before the polish

Everything downstream — cover, title, blurb, categories, ads — depends on which subgenre you're claiming. Claim one lane, deliberately, based on where the book's heart is and what the lane's market looks like (start with what romance subgenres sell and your lane's live snapshot). A book positioned between two lanes inherits the competition of both and the browsers of neither.

STEP 2

Lead with one trope promise

Your lead trope is the hook readers repeat to each other — the answer to “what's it about?” that fits in four words. Supporting tropes deepen the read, but the packaging should shout exactly one promise. Choosing that lead from demand-and-supply evidence rather than instinct is the whole subject of how to research tropes before writing.

STEP 3

Set the heat level on purpose — and signal it

Heat is one of the sharpest segmentations in romance: sweet readers and spicy readers browse past each other, and each lane carries its own expectation of how hot a book in it runs. Missignal heat and you collect the wrong readers, whose reviews then warn your right readers away. Decide the temperature, then make cover, blurb vocabulary, and category placement all announce it consistently. (A Tropesmith Map states the spice expectation for your lane outright.)

STEP 4

Package to the lane's conventions — then differentiate one notch

Every lane has a cover and title language: the styles, type, and imagery its readers have learned to trust as a signal of “this is my kind of book.” Study the current top covers in your lane and match the conventions — then differentiate by one deliberate notch, not three. A cover that ignores its lane's language doesn't read as fresh; it reads as not for me. This is why a Map's cover direction is drawn from real top covers in your lane, not from general design taste.

STEP 5

Write the blurb in reader vocabulary

The words readers use — on their Goodreads shelves, in reviews, in BookTok captions — are the words that make your blurb feel instantly right and your keywords findable. Harvest the recurring phrases from your lane and comps rather than inventing elegant synonyms; the reader's own vocabulary is the most persuasive language available, and it's free. (Where to find it: reader demand signals.)

STEP 6

Place it: categories and comps

Positioning becomes concrete in two choices: the shelves you claim (see Kindle romance categories) and the comp titles you name and target ads against (see finding comparable titles). Both should follow from the lane, trope, and heat decisions above — if they feel hard, positioning upstream is still vague.

Write it down: the positioning statement

Compress the six decisions into one sentence you can test everything against:

Template

For readers of [comp titles] who love [lead trope], this is a [heat level] [subgenre] about [protagonists in tension], where [the promise paid off].

If the cover designer, the blurb, the ad audience, and the category picks all serve that sentence, the book is positioned. If any element needs a different sentence to justify it, that element is off-position — fix it before launch, when it's cheap.

Positioning is evidence work

Notice what every step needed: current knowledge of your lane's bestsellers, its readers' vocabulary, its cover language, its heat norms. None of it is guessable from inside your manuscript. Frequently asked questions

What does positioning mean for a romance novel?

It's the instantly-readable promise of what the book is: which subgenre it lives in, which lead trope it delivers, how hot it runs, and what feeling it pays off — communicated consistently by the cover, title, blurb, categories, and comps so the right reader recognises the book at a glance.

When should I think about positioning — before or after writing?

Before, ideally: choosing the lane, lead trope, and heat level first means the market can inform the book while changes are free. If the book is already written, position honestly around what it truly delivers rather than what you wish it delivered — mispositioned promises collect the wrong readers and reviews.

Why is my well-reviewed book not selling?

Often a positioning gap rather than a quality problem: packaging that doesn't match the lane's conventions, an unclear lead-trope promise, mismatched heat signals, or categories the wrong browsers use. Check each element against a one-sentence positioning statement and against your lane's current top sellers.
